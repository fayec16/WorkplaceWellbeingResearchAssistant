## 1. Occupational burnout, sources of work-related stress, and Effort-Reward imbalance among hospital-based radiographers in Indonesia: A nationwide survey.
*Radiography (London, England : 1995), 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42541970/)

**Summary:** A nationwide survey of 511 hospital radiographers in Indonesia found a 47.2% burnout prevalence. Key organizational factors associated with burnout included poor job control, lack of manager and peer support, unclear role expectations, shift work patterns, and absence of psychological counseling services. Rewards were generally seen as commensurate with effort, pointing to structural and relational factors—rather than pay—as the primary levers.

**Try this today:** This week, assess your own role clarity: write down your top 3 responsibilities and check whether your manager would list the same ones. If there's ambiguity, schedule a 15-minute conversation to align expectations—role clarity is one of the most actionable burnout protective factors an individual can address directly.

## 2. Associations of workplace violence with depression and anxiety among health-care workers: the mediating role of perceived work stress.
*Global health action, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42529960/)

**Summary:** A large cross-sectional study of nearly 40,000 Chinese healthcare workers found that exposure to workplace violence was strongly associated with higher odds of depression (OR=3.79) and anxiety (OR=3.81). Perceived work stress partially mediated these relationships, explaining roughly 16–19% of the association, suggesting that violence raises stress which in turn damages mental health. The findings call for integrated organizational policies addressing both violence prevention and stress management.

**Try this today:** If you've recently experienced a hostile or aggressive interaction at work (from a client, patient, or colleague), proactively reach out to your manager or HR this week to document and discuss it—early reporting reduces the chronic stress accumulation that mediates the path from workplace aggression to anxiety and depression.

## 3. Occupational Stress in the Neuroticism-Burnout Link: An Exploratory Examination of Physiological Contributions.
*Clinical psychopharmacology and neuroscience : the official scientific journal of the Korean College of Neuropsychopharmacology, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42528350/)

**Summary:** A study of 150 Korean workers in high-stress jobs found that neuroticism predicts occupational burnout primarily through perceived occupational stress as a mediator, not through physiological pathways. Workers higher in neuroticism perceive more stress, which in turn drives burnout; age was inversely associated with burnout. Wearable-derived autonomic flexibility measures trended in the expected direction but did not reach statistical significance.

**Try this today:** If you recognize yourself as prone to negative emotional reactivity (neuroticism), try a brief daily 'stress audit': at the end of each workday this week, write one sentence naming your dominant stressor. Externalizing perceived stress rather than ruminating has been shown to reduce its amplifying effect on burnout-related exhaustion.

## 4. Mental health first aid in the workplace: a reflexive thematic analysis of UK workers' experiences.
*International journal of qualitative studies on health and well-being, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42482327/)

**Summary:** A qualitative study embedded in a UK randomized trial found that employees were skeptical of Mental Health First Aid (MHFA) programs at work, citing ambiguity about the role's purpose, fear of stigma, and concerns that disclosure could harm career progression or workplace relationships. MHFA was seen as most valuable when backed by a genuine broader organizational commitment to wellbeing—not as a standalone fix. Crucially, employees felt MHFA did not address the root organizational causes of distress.

**Try this today:** Before referring a struggling colleague to your company's MHFA resource this week, pair it with a concrete act of organizational support—such as advocating to your manager for a workload adjustment or flexible arrangement—so the person receives both emotional first aid and a signal that structural stressors are being taken seriously.

## 5. Burnout as Social Information: How Coworker Burnout Level and Coworker Burnout Consistency Interact With Mastery Goal Orientation to Affect Work Performance.
*Stress and health : journal of the International Society for the Investigation of Stress, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42473049/)

**Summary:** A study using conservation of resources theory found that when a high proportion of teammates are burned out (high consistency of coworker burnout), employees reduce their own work effort and performance—unless they have a strong mastery goal orientation (intrinsic drive to learn and improve). The effect was not simply burnout 'contagion' but a social information cue that the environment is depleted. Mastery-oriented workers were buffered against this pull.

**Try this today:** If you notice your team seems collectively exhausted, consciously reconnect with a learning goal this week—pick one skill or process you want to improve and frame at least one task around developing it. Research suggests this mastery orientation acts as a personal resource that protects effort and performance even in burned-out team environments.

## 6. Social comparison contributes to work exhaustion in the context of workplace AI use: A three-wave follow-up study of Finnish workers.
*SSM - population health, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42471855/)

**Summary:** A three-wave longitudinal study of over 2,100 Finnish workers found that frequent AI use at work was not itself associated with burnout, but high social comparison tendency was robustly linked to greater exhaustion at both the individual and population levels. Workers who perceived themselves as AI-ready reported significantly lower exhaustion. Exploratory analysis found that among highly comparison-oriented people, frequent AI use did elevate exhaustion.

**Try this today:** This week, notice when you're benchmarking your AI skill level or productivity against colleagues. Try replacing one instance of upward social comparison ('they use AI better than I do') with a self-referenced benchmark ('I'm doing X better than I did last month')—reducing social comparison orientation is a directly modifiable predictor of work exhaustion.

## 7. Biological Markers of Burnout Severity: A Systematic Review.
*Stress and health : journal of the International Society for the Investigation of Stress, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42452902/)

**Summary:** A systematic review of 111 studies examining 36 biomarkers found no consistent physiological signature for burnout syndrome across the HPA axis, immune system, or cardiovascular parameters, with the partial exception of HbA1c and blood glucose showing slightly more consistent associations. The authors conclude that current biological evidence cannot support routine clinical diagnosis of burnout via biomarkers. This underscores the need for behavioral and organizational—rather than purely biological—approaches to burnout identification.

**Try this today:** Rather than waiting for a biomarker test to confirm burnout risk, use a validated self-report screen this week—such as the single-item burnout question ('How often do you feel burned out from your work?') or the Oldenburg Burnout Inventory—to get an actionable baseline you can track over time.

## 8. Drivers of Professional Satisfaction in Emergency Medicine Residency Training in the US.
*AEM education and training, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42428348/)

**Summary:** A national survey of nearly 4,000 US emergency medicine residents identified the top drivers of professional satisfaction: team culture and community, positive patient impact, and work-life balance—all outranking financial compensation. The most prevalent themes were consistent across demographic groups, suggesting these social and meaning-based factors are broadly important rather than niche. The findings argue for actively cultivating team relationships and meaning as burnout antidotes.

**Try this today:** This week, spend 5 minutes writing down one concrete way your work positively impacted a specific person or outcome recently. Research on meaning-based coping suggests that deliberately recalling moments of positive patient or client impact replenishes the sense of purpose that buffers against emotional exhaustion.

## 9. Causal mediation analysis of contact with COVID-19 patients and burnout syndrome in physiotherapists in Japan: secondary analysis of a multicentre observational study.
*European journal of psychotraumatology, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42496126/)

**Summary:** A causal mediation analysis of Japanese physiotherapists found that the link between direct COVID-19 patient contact and burnout operated mainly through a behavioral mediator—feeling like quitting or taking a break—rather than directly. Receiving recognition and respect from the organization nearly eliminated this indirect pathway, while resource strain (inadequate staffing/reimbursement) amplified it. This highlights authentic recognition as a modifiable organizational lever against burnout.

**Try this today:** Give a specific, genuine acknowledgment to one colleague this week—name what they did and why it mattered—rather than a generic 'good job.' The research suggests that felt recognition directly buffers the stress-to-burnout pathway, and this costs nothing to implement.

## 10. Creating a culture of well-being: How a committee transformed a hospital.
*Nursing management, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42475238/)

**Summary:** A hospital wellness committee at a 341-bed Magnet facility reduced nurse burnout by implementing a coordinated set of low-cost, multi-domain interventions including hydration stations, serenity spaces, therapy dog visits, stress management training, and enhanced security—all guided by staff survey data and supported by shared governance. The key success factors were strong leadership backing and empowering frontline staff to co-design solutions. The initiative shows that coordinated small changes compound into culture shift.

**Try this today:** Run a quick informal 'two-question pulse check' with your immediate team this week: 'What's one thing draining your energy right now?' and 'What's one low-cost change that would help?' Aggregating even informal input before proposing wellbeing changes mirrors the evidence-based committee process that drove this hospital's success.
