## 1. Occupational risk factors for suicide: A systematic review and meta-analysis.
*Medicine, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42566567/)

**Summary:** A systematic review and meta-analysis found that occupational factors including job demands, job strain, workplace bullying, burnout, and work-family conflict are significant risk factors for suicide across dimensions (ideation, attempts, completion). Job demands alone showed an odds ratio of 1.29 for suicide-related outcomes, underscoring the serious mental health consequences of toxic work environments. The findings call for active mental health monitoring and screening in workplace health policies.

**Try this today:** This week, check in with at least one colleague who may be showing signs of chronic stress or withdrawal — and look up your organization's Employee Assistance Program (EAP) contact so you have it ready to share or use.

## 2. The Impact of Organizational Climate Toward Sexual Minority Workers on Burnout: The Mediating Role of Quality of Work Life.
*New solutions : a journal of environmental and occupational health policy : NS, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42565777/)

**Summary:** A study of 256 Portuguese and Brazilian workers found that an inclusive organizational climate toward sexual minority employees reduced burnout both directly and indirectly, largely by improving quality of work life (QoWL). Workers in more inclusive climates reported better general well-being and working conditions. QoWL fully mediated part of the climate-burnout relationship, suggesting that inclusion efforts protect against exhaustion for all workers.

**Try this today:** Advocate for or participate in one concrete inclusion initiative at your workplace this week — such as joining or promoting an ERG (Employee Resource Group), or raising inclusive language norms in a team meeting.

## 3. The impact of Solution Focused Practice on workplace wellbeing: a systematic review.
*Journal of interprofessional care, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42565667/)

**Summary:** A systematic review of 11 studies found that Solution Focused Practice (SFP) — a strengths-based, collaborative approach — positively impacted workplace wellbeing across medical, health, and social care settings. Key benefits included enhanced work fulfillment, improved emotional wellbeing, better alignment with personal values, and stronger relationships with colleagues and service users. The approach shifts focus from problems to resources and what is already working.

**Try this today:** In your next one-on-one or team meeting, try a solution-focused question such as 'What has been working well this week that we should do more of?' instead of leading with problems or pain points.

## 4. Associations of workplace violence with depression and anxiety among health-care workers: the mediating role of perceived work stress.
*Global health action, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42529960/)

**Summary:** A large-scale cross-sectional study of nearly 40,000 healthcare workers in China found that exposure to workplace violence was strongly associated with higher odds of depression (OR=3.79) and anxiety (OR=3.81). Perceived work stress partially mediated these associations, accounting for roughly 16–19% of the effect. The findings highlight that stress perception is an important mechanism linking adverse workplace conditions to poor mental health.

**Try this today:** If you experience or witness disrespectful or hostile behavior at work, document it and report it through your organization's formal channels this week — and make use of any available stress management or counseling resources before the stress compounds.

## 5. Occupational Stress in the Neuroticism-Burnout Link: An Exploratory Examination of Physiological Contributions.
*Clinical psychopharmacology and neuroscience : the official scientific journal of the Korean College of Neuropsychopharmacology, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42528350/)

**Summary:** A study of 150 Korean workers in high-stress occupations found that neuroticism increases burnout primarily through perceived occupational stress — not directly through physiological pathways. Workers who perceive their job demands as more stressful are at significantly higher burnout risk, and wearable-measured autonomic flexibility (HRV) showed a directional but non-significant trend as an additional pathway. Age was inversely associated with burnout, suggesting some protective effect of career experience.

**Try this today:** This week, practice reappraising one recurring work stressor by writing down a brief alternative interpretation of the demand (e.g., 'this deadline is tight' reframed as 'this is a chance to practice prioritization') to actively reduce perceived stress rather than waiting for workload to decrease.

## 6. Mental health first aid in the workplace: a reflexive thematic analysis of UK workers' experiences.
*International journal of qualitative studies on health and well-being, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42482327/)

**Summary:** A qualitative study embedded in a UK randomized trial found that workplace Mental Health First Aid (MHFA) programs are undermined by ambiguity about their purpose, stigma concerns, and employees' fear that disclosing mental health struggles could harm their careers or relationships. Participants were skeptical that MHFA addressed the real organizational causes of distress. The research suggests MHFA works best as part of a broader, genuine organizational commitment to wellbeing rather than as a standalone initiative.

**Try this today:** If your organization has a Mental Health First Aider or wellbeing champion, find out this week exactly what that role covers and its confidentiality boundaries — knowing this removes a key barrier to using support when you actually need it.

## 7. Social comparison contributes to work exhaustion in the context of workplace AI use: A three-wave follow-up study of Finnish workers.
*SSM - population health, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42471855/)

**Summary:** A three-wave longitudinal study of over 2,000 Finnish workers found that frequent AI use at work was not independently linked to exhaustion, but social comparison tendency robustly predicted higher burnout at both within- and between-person levels. Notably, among workers who frequently compare themselves to colleagues, high AI use was associated with elevated exhaustion. Perceived AI readiness, however, acted as a protective resource — workers who felt confident about AI reported lower exhaustion.

**Try this today:** This week, take stock of how often you compare your output or pace to AI-assisted colleagues and consciously redirect that energy into one concrete AI skill-building activity (e.g., a 20-minute tutorial on a tool relevant to your role) to build genuine readiness rather than anxiety.

## 8. When violence meets leadership: Leadership climate as a buffer in the link between violence, harmful alcohol use, and burnout among healthcare workers in Sweden.
*International journal of nursing studies advances, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42396545/)

**Summary:** A two-wave longitudinal study of 2,446 Swedish healthcare workers found that a strong leadership climate buffered the link between workplace violence and harmful alcohol use, and subsequently reduced burnout. Under weak leadership conditions, violence predicted burnout through increased harmful drinking; under moderate or strong leadership, this indirect pathway was non-significant. The findings highlight leadership quality as a meaningful organizational lever for protecting staff exposed to violence.

**Try this today:** If you are in a leadership role, schedule a brief check-in this week with any team members who may have experienced stressful or hostile incidents — a simple, direct conversation signals psychological safety and has measurable protective effects.
