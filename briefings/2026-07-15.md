## 1. Exercise frequency and physician burnout among colorectal surgeons in the USA: should we be worried?
*Techniques in coloproctology, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42448839/)

**Summary:** A survey of 208 colorectal surgeons in the US found that 77% reported burnout symptoms in the past 6 months, with over half feeling overwhelmed and 69% experiencing anxiety, depression, or irritability. Surgeons who exercised less frequently were significantly more likely to report feeling overwhelmed, suggesting a protective role of physical activity against at least some burnout symptoms. The cross-sectional design limits causal conclusions, but the association between exercise and reduced overwhelm was statistically significant.

**Try this today:** Schedule at least one 30-minute exercise session this week as a deliberate burnout buffer — block it on your calendar as a non-negotiable appointment, the same way you would a meeting.

## 2. Tailoring the unified protocol for the treatment of burnout: A co-design approach.
*Acta psychologica, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42447580/)

**Summary:** Researchers adapted an evidence-based cognitive-behavioral therapy framework (the Unified Protocol) into a group-based burnout intervention using co-design with both burnout sufferers and clinicians. Key additions included a values-based module and a component on cognitive functioning, reflecting the lived realities of people experiencing burnout. The resulting protocol (UP-B) offers a more tailored, structured approach to treating burnout than generic stress interventions.

**Try this today:** Take 10 minutes today to write down your top three personal or professional values, then note one way your current work either aligns with or contradicts each — this values-clarification exercise mirrors a core component of the new burnout protocol.

## 3. Association of occupational burnout and perceived healthcare service quality with medical disputes among physicians and medical technicians: a cross-sectional study in China.
*BMC health services research, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42443883/)

**Summary:** A cross-sectional study of 1,570 physicians and medical technicians in China found that higher occupational burnout was significantly associated with greater likelihood of experiencing medical disputes, while higher perceived healthcare service quality reduced dispute risk. Working more than 40 hours per week and seeing high patient volumes compounded the risk. The findings suggest burnout has downstream consequences not just for workers but for patient safety and institutional outcomes.

**Try this today:** If you are in a leadership or management role, audit one team member's current workload this week and identify a single task or meeting that could be reduced, delegated, or eliminated to lower burnout risk.

## 4. Distress Profiles and Help-Seeking Behaviors Among Diverse Healthcare Workers Participating in a Mental Health Screening Program.
*Academic psychiatry : the journal of the American Association of Directors of Psychiatric Residency Training and the Association for Academic Psychiatry, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42443545/)

**Summary:** An analysis of over 6,200 healthcare workers at a large academic medical center found that Asian American/Pacific Islander, Hispanic/Latino, and multiracial employees carried a disproportionate burden of depression, loneliness, and suicide risk. Workers who declined to disclose their race/ethnicity showed the highest levels of burnout and hopelessness, suggesting that non-disclosure itself may signal distress and distrust. The findings underscore the need for culturally inclusive mental health resources in workplace wellbeing programs.

**Try this today:** Review your organization's employee mental health resources this week to check whether they are offered in multiple languages and explicitly signal cultural competence — if gaps exist, flag them to HR or your wellbeing team.

## 5. Servant leadership and job satisfaction in medical assistants: mediating effect of work-life balance and burnout.
*Journal of health organization and management, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42441939/)

**Summary:** A large study of nearly 1,000 medical assistants found that servant leadership — where leaders prioritize the needs of their team — significantly improved job satisfaction, and that this effect was partially mediated by better work-life balance and reduced burnout. Both work-life balance and burnout independently connected leadership style to satisfaction outcomes, validating the Job Demands-Resources model in this healthcare context. The research suggests that leadership behavior is a lever for reducing burnout, not just individual coping strategies.

**Try this today:** This week, identify one specific way you can act as a 'servant leader' toward a colleague or direct report — such as proactively removing an obstacle for them or publicly recognizing their contribution — and do it before Friday.

## 6. Feeling of Guilt, Stress, and Self-Compassion Among Nursing Staff in the Workplace.
*Research in nursing & health, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42439811/)

**Summary:** A survey of 179 nursing staff found that interpersonal guilt at work was strongly linked to higher stress and lower self-compassion, with guilt occurring on average more than seven days per month. The main triggers were time shortages and understaffing, and common coping strategies included communication and emotional suppression. Notably, self-compassion was associated with less stress, even though it did not buffer the guilt-stress relationship directly.

**Try this today:** Practice a brief self-compassion exercise after a stressful work moment this week: acknowledge the difficulty, remind yourself that mistakes and stress are part of shared human experience, and offer yourself the same kindness you would give a colleague in the same situation.

## 7. Investigating languishing in North American genetic counselors.
*Journal of genetic counseling, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42438186/)

**Summary:** A qualitative study of genetic counselors found that many experienced 'languishing' — a state distinct from clinical burnout, characterized by apathy, low motivation, and feeling replaceable — often linked to feeling like an outsider at work and a mismatch between career expectations and reality. Intersectionality and identity-related workplace experiences contributed significantly to this state. The findings highlight that wellbeing interventions need to address this 'middle ground' of mental health, not just clinical burnout or depression.

**Try this today:** Reflect on whether you feel more 'languishing' (going through the motions, low energy, not quite ill but not thriving) rather than burned out, and if so, identify one small source of meaning or engagement you could reintroduce into your work routine this week — even a 15-minute project you care about.

## 8. Drivers of Professional Satisfaction in Emergency Medicine Residency Training in the US.
*AEM education and training, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42428348/)

**Summary:** A national survey of nearly 4,000 emergency medicine residents identified the top drivers of professional satisfaction as team culture, positive patient impact, and work-life balance — factors that clustered around people and purpose rather than pay or prestige. These themes were consistent across demographic groups, suggesting universal value in relational and meaningful work. The findings imply that organizations focused only on reducing burnout may miss the positive side of wellbeing: actively building satisfaction.

**Try this today:** Write down the single biggest source of meaning or satisfaction in your current job — then schedule one activity this week that directly connects to it, treating it as a wellbeing investment rather than a nice-to-have.

## 9. Evolving Well-Being and Personal Resource Profiles Following a Smartphone App Intervention for Stress Management.
*Stress and health : journal of the International Society for the Investigation of Stress, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42411755/)

**Summary:** A three-week study of 241 participants using a smartphone stress-management app (WellBe!) found that users with the most difficulty managing stress showed the greatest probability of shifting to better coping profiles after the intervention. Changes were gradual — moving between adjacent profiles rather than dramatic jumps — and were not observed in the control group. This suggests that even brief digital interventions can meaningfully shift wellbeing trajectories for those most at risk.

**Try this today:** Download a free evidence-based stress or mindfulness app (such as Headspace, Calm, or Wysa) and commit to using it for just five minutes each day for one week, treating it as an experiment in your own wellbeing.

## 10. When violence meets leadership: Leadership climate as a buffer in the link between violence, harmful alcohol use, and burnout among healthcare workers in Sweden.
*International journal of nursing studies advances, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42396545/)

**Summary:** A longitudinal study of 2,446 Swedish healthcare workers found that workplace violence predicted harmful alcohol use, which in turn predicted burnout — but a strong leadership climate significantly weakened the violence-to-alcohol-use link. The buffering effect of good leadership was especially pronounced under weaker supervisory conditions, and the indirect path from violence to burnout via alcohol was only significant when leadership support was low. This highlights leadership quality as a structural protective factor against burnout, not just individual resilience.

**Try this today:** If you are a manager, schedule a brief one-on-one check-in with each team member this week specifically to ask how they are coping with stressors at work — the act of attentive leadership itself is a measurable protective factor against burnout.

## 11. Pathways linking emergency department work environment to nurse's burnout: The mediating effects of teamwork and workload.
*International emergency nursing, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42364448/)

**Summary:** A survey of 156 emergency department nurses in South Korea found that a better nursing work environment reduced burnout both directly and indirectly by improving teamwork and reducing perceived workload. Teamwork and workload each independently mediated the environment-burnout relationship, though the sequential pathway from teamwork to workload was not significant. The study underscores that burnout prevention requires addressing organizational conditions, not just building individual resilience.

**Try this today:** Identify one recurring friction point in your team's workflow this week — a poorly run meeting, unclear responsibility, or communication gap — and propose one specific fix to your manager or team, framing it as a workload and wellbeing intervention.

## 12. Vicarious trauma and burnout: the role of self-compassion.
*European journal of psychotraumatology, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42301724/)

**Summary:** A survey of 255 practitioner psychologists in the UK found that higher vicarious trauma from exposure to clients' trauma stories was strongly associated with higher burnout and lower self-compassion. While self-compassion did not statistically moderate the trauma-burnout relationship at the group level, sensitivity analysis suggested that self-kindness specifically may weaken this link. The findings support routine screening for vicarious trauma and burnout in trauma-exposed professions and point to compassion-focused approaches as a potential protective strategy.

**Try this today:** If your work involves frequent exposure to others' distress or difficult content, set a specific end-of-workday boundary ritual this week — such as a short walk, journaling, or a transition phrase — to cognitively separate from vicarious stress before entering personal time.

## 13. Professional quality of life of hospital nurses: A cross-sectional study.
*International journal of nursing studies advances, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42294109/)

**Summary:** A cross-sectional study of over 1,000 nurses across 10 Dutch hospitals found that perceived social support from family and friends was significantly associated with lower burnout and secondary traumatic stress, and higher compassion satisfaction. Fear of making clinical errors was independently linked to higher burnout and STS. The results suggest that nurturing social connections outside work and reducing error-related anxiety are important protective factors for occupational mental health.

**Try this today:** Reach out to one friend or family member this week — not to vent about work, but to genuinely connect — as maintaining outside social support is empirically linked to lower workplace burnout.

## 14. Training climate matters: programmatic openness, individual belonging, and trainee mistreatment in graduate medical education.
*Medical education online, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42286872/)

**Summary:** A survey of 266 medical residents across 62 graduate medical education programs found that both programmatic openness (a transparent, supportive training climate) and individual sense of belonging independently protected against perceived mistreatment and burnout. Importantly, programmatic openness had its strongest buffering effect for trainees who felt low belonging, suggesting a compensatory mechanism where a good organizational climate can partially compensate for individuals who feel less connected. Burnout was lowest when both openness and belonging were present.

**Try this today:** If you manage or mentor others, take one visible action this week to signal psychological safety in your team — such as openly acknowledging uncertainty, welcoming a dissenting opinion, or publicly thanking someone for raising a concern.

## 15. Burnout and Work-Life Balance: A Longitudinal Study Into the Transition From Medical School to Postgraduate Training.
*Deutsches Arzteblatt international, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42273790/)

**Summary:** A longitudinal German study tracking 321 medical students through postgraduate training found that emotional exhaustion rose sharply — from affecting 10% of students to 47% of first-year postgraduate trainees — while work-life balance declined steadily across the transition. Personal accomplishment scores were low throughout and continued to fall, suggesting structural rather than individual causes. The findings highlight that early-career professional transitions are a critical window for burnout prevention.

**Try this today:** If you are navigating a major career transition or new role, proactively protect one work-life boundary this week that you have recently let slip — such as a firm stop time, a lunch break away from your desk, or a protected evening activity.

## 16. A longitudinal network analysis of social connection and professional quality of life among healthcare workers during the COVID-19 pandemic.
*Journal of affective disorders, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42259489/)

**Summary:** A three-wave longitudinal survey of 230 US healthcare workers during the COVID-19 pandemic found that loneliness and positive mental health had the strongest downstream influence on all other wellbeing and social outcomes over time. Giving support to others had a mutually reinforcing relationship with positive mental health: those who supported colleagues at one time point reported better mental health and work satisfaction later, which in turn predicted more support-giving. This bidirectional 'helping loop' suggests that acts of workplace support benefit the giver as much as the receiver.

**Try this today:** Offer a concrete act of support to a colleague this week — such as helping with a task, sharing a useful resource, or simply checking in — knowing that giving support is empirically linked to your own improved mental health and work satisfaction over time.
