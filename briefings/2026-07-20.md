## 1. Social comparison contributes to work exhaustion in the context of workplace AI use: A three-wave follow-up study of Finnish workers.
*SSM - population health, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42471855/)

**Summary:** A large longitudinal study of Finnish workers found that social comparison tendencies — not AI use itself — are the main driver of work exhaustion in AI-augmented workplaces. Workers who felt more prepared for AI reported lower exhaustion over time, and among those prone to social comparison, frequent AI use was associated with higher burnout. This suggests that individual psychological dispositions matter more than the technology itself.

**Try this today:** This week, notice when you catch yourself comparing your AI proficiency or productivity to colleagues. When that urge arises, redirect attention to your own progress: write down one concrete skill you've developed or improved recently to ground your self-assessment in personal growth rather than social comparison.

## 2. Factors associated with quiet quitting among public health specialists in Trkiye: a cross-sectional study.
*BMC public health, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42471649/)

**Summary:** A cross-sectional study of public health specialists in Turkey found that 'quiet quitting' — passive disengagement at work — was strongly associated with professional hopelessness and a mismatch between one's training and actual job role. Workers without administrative authority and those who felt their specialty had a bleak future were significantly more likely to disengage quietly. This highlights role clarity and career outlook as key levers for engagement.

**Try this today:** Schedule a 20-minute conversation with your manager this week to clarify how your current role connects to your longer-term career path. Come prepared with one specific area where your skills feel underutilized, and ask how you might take on a task or project that better uses your expertise.

## 3. Feasibility and acceptability of a joint employee-manager workplace intervention to support mental health and job retention (MENTOR 2.0).
*BMC public health, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42471615/)

**Summary:** A feasibility study of the MENTOR 2.0 intervention found that joint employee-manager sessions — facilitated by a trained liaison worker — led to reductions in psychological distress and improvements in productivity, work engagement, and psychological flexibility. Participants particularly valued the opportunity for open, confidential dialogue with their line managers. The study highlights that mental health support embedded in the manager-employee relationship is more effective than employee-only interventions.

**Try this today:** Initiate a low-stakes, one-on-one check-in with your direct manager this week focused specifically on wellbeing rather than task status — frame it as 'how is the work working for me' rather than 'what work am I doing.' Even a 15-minute conversation about workload or interpersonal dynamics can open the kind of dialogue that MENTOR 2.0 found beneficial.

## 4. Biological Markers of Burnout Severity: A Systematic Review.
*Stress and health : journal of the International Society for the Investigation of Stress, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42452902/)

**Summary:** A systematic review of 111 studies found no consistent biological signature for burnout syndrome — markers like cortisol and immune indicators showed inconsistent associations across studies. HbA1c and blood glucose showed the most consistent links but evidence remains inconclusive. This underscores that burnout cannot yet be reliably detected through routine biological screening and must still be assessed through validated psychological tools.

**Try this today:** Rather than waiting for physical symptoms to signal burnout, use a validated self-assessment tool this week — such as the free Maslach Burnout Inventory General Survey items available online — to get a baseline reading of your exhaustion, cynicism, and efficacy levels, and note any changes over coming weeks.

## 5. Evolving Well-Being and Personal Resource Profiles Following a Smartphone App Intervention for Stress Management.
*Stress and health : journal of the International Society for the Investigation of Stress, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42411755/)

**Summary:** A three-week study of a stress management smartphone app (WellBe!) found that users who started with poorer wellbeing and higher stress were most likely to shift into better-coping profiles when using the app daily, while control group profiles remained stable. Changes were gradual, moving between adjacent wellbeing states rather than dramatic jumps. This suggests digital micro-interventions can meaningfully move the needle for those who need it most.

**Try this today:** If you are currently experiencing elevated work stress, commit to using a well-being or mindfulness app (e.g., Headspace, Calm, or a free option like Insight Timer) daily for one week — even just 5–10 minutes — and track your stress level each morning on a simple 1–10 scale to observe any gradual shift.

## 6. When violence meets leadership: Leadership climate as a buffer in the link between violence, harmful alcohol use, and burnout among healthcare workers in Sweden.
*International journal of nursing studies advances, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42396545/)

**Summary:** A two-wave panel study of nearly 2,500 Swedish healthcare workers found that a supportive leadership climate weakened the link between workplace violence and harmful alcohol use, and also reduced the downstream path from violence to burnout. The protective effect of good leadership was especially evident under weaker leadership conditions, where violence most strongly predicted later burnout. Even modest improvements in supervisory support may break the violence-to-burnout chain.

**Try this today:** If you manage others or have influence over team culture, pick one concrete act of supportive leadership this week — such as explicitly acknowledging a stressful event your team experienced, checking in individually with someone who seemed affected, or publicly validating a team member's effort after a difficult situation.

## 7. Mental Workload and Burnout among Social Work Professionals in Spain: The Protective Role of Psychological Capital and Perceived Social Support.
*Health & social work, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42276977/)

**Summary:** A study of 270 Spanish social workers found that high mental workload predicts burnout, but both psychological capital (optimism, resilience, self-efficacy, hope) and perceived social support partially buffer this relationship. Even when workload is high, workers with stronger internal resources and external support reported lower burnout. This points to building personal resources as a parallel strategy alongside workload reduction.

**Try this today:** This week, identify one source of social support at work you have been underusing — a trusted colleague, a peer group, or a manager — and reach out for a brief informal conversation about how things are going. Simultaneously, write down one recent work win, however small, to actively reinforce your sense of self-efficacy.

## 8. A longitudinal network analysis of social connection and professional quality of life among healthcare workers during the COVID-19 pandemic.
*Journal of affective disorders, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42259489/)

**Summary:** A three-wave longitudinal network study of US healthcare workers during COVID-19 found that loneliness and positive mental health were the strongest upstream predictors of all other psychological and social outcomes, including burnout. Giving social support had a mutually reinforcing relationship with positive mental health — those who gave support at wave 1 had better mental health and work satisfaction at wave 2, which in turn led to more support-giving at wave 3. This suggests that actively supporting others at work is itself a protective wellbeing behavior.

**Try this today:** Proactively offer support to one colleague this week — this could be as simple as checking in on someone who seems stressed, helping with a task, or sending a short encouraging message. Research suggests that the act of giving support predicts your own improved wellbeing and work satisfaction over time.

## 9. Sustained improvements in radiologist engagement and administrative alignment following structured leadership and wellness interventions: A single institution three-year observational study.
*Clinical imaging, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42202539/)

**Summary:** A three-year observational study at a multi-campus radiology department found that six structured leadership interventions — including weekly rounding with closed-loop follow-up, listening forums, dedicated check-ins for remote workers, and integrating physicians into governance — were associated with large increases in radiologist engagement and administrative alignment, moving from the 22nd to the 63rd percentile. Gains far exceeded system-wide trends, suggesting that consistent, transparent, two-way communication structures drive sustained engagement.

**Try this today:** If you are in a leadership or team-lead role, introduce one 'closed-loop' communication habit this week: when a team member raises a concern or suggestion, follow up explicitly with 'here is what we did about what you said' — even if the answer is 'we can't act on this yet, and here's why.' This transparency is a low-cost, high-impact engagement driver.
