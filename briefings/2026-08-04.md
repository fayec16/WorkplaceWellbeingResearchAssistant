## 1. Sources of stress among long-term care workers: Findings from qualitative and survey data.
*PloS one, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42546029/)

**Summary:** A study of long-term care workers found that time pressures, difficult work situations, and financial stress are the primary sources of occupational stress, with personal and family responsibilities adding to the burden. Social support networks — including friends, family, and colleagues — served as meaningful buffers against stress, alongside coping strategies like mindfulness and prayer. The findings highlight both organizational and personal levers for stress management in demanding care roles.

**Try this today:** This week, intentionally reach out to one colleague or friend to share a current work stressor — treating social connection as a deliberate stress-management tool rather than an incidental one.

## 2. Provider well-being in the intensive virtual evidence-based psychotherapy team: A program description with preliminary evaluation data in the Veterans Health Administration.
*Psychological services, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42545338/)

**Summary:** A virtual, team-based psychotherapy program at the VA health system was intentionally designed to support provider wellbeing at organizational, team, and individual levels. In a small preliminary evaluation, staff reported lower burnout, secondary traumatic stress, and moral distress after joining the program, with positive feedback on collegial support and personal wellness accountability. This suggests that embedding wellbeing supports directly into clinical program design — rather than offering them as add-ons — can make a meaningful difference.

**Try this today:** Identify one recurring work structure (a weekly meeting, project rhythm, or team check-in) and propose adding a brief, explicit wellbeing component — such as a 5-minute round of 'what's draining / what's energizing' — to make support a built-in feature rather than an afterthought.

## 3. Occupational Stress in the Neuroticism-Burnout Link: An Exploratory Examination of Physiological Contributions.
*Clinical psychopharmacology and neuroscience : the official scientific journal of the Korean College of Neuropsychopharmacology, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42528350/)

**Summary:** A study of 150 Korean workers in high-stress jobs found that neuroticism increases burnout primarily by amplifying perceived occupational stress, which then drives burnout. Wearable physiological measures (EEG and heart rate variability) showed that reduced autonomic flexibility accompanied higher stress, though this pathway was not statistically significant. Age was inversely associated with burnout, suggesting experience or life perspective may be protective.

**Try this today:** Try a brief daily stress-perception check-in: at the end of each workday this week, rate your perceived stress level (1–10) and note one specific trigger — building awareness of stress appraisal patterns is a first step toward interrupting the neuroticism-to-burnout pathway.

## 4. Mental health first aid in the workplace: a reflexive thematic analysis of UK workers' experiences.
*International journal of qualitative studies on health and well-being, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42482327/)

**Summary:** A qualitative study of UK employees found that Mental Health First Aid (MHFA) programs face significant barriers including ambiguity about their purpose, concerns about stigma, and fears that disclosing mental health struggles could harm workplace relationships or career progression. Participants felt MHFA worked best when embedded within broader organizational commitment to wellbeing rather than as a standalone initiative. Simply having trained first aiders is insufficient if underlying organizational stressors are not also addressed.

**Try this today:** If your organization has a mental health first aider or EAP resource, take 10 minutes today to look up exactly what confidentiality protections apply — knowing the boundaries clearly can reduce the disclosure anxiety that prevents people from using available support.

## 5. Social comparison contributes to work exhaustion in the context of workplace AI use: A three-wave follow-up study of Finnish workers.
*SSM - population health, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42471855/)

**Summary:** A three-wave longitudinal study of Finnish workers found that frequent AI use at work was not directly associated with work exhaustion, but social comparison tendencies — consistently measuring oneself against colleagues — were robustly linked to higher exhaustion at both individual and population levels. Workers who felt more prepared for AI reported lower exhaustion, and exploratory analyses suggested that among high social-comparers, frequent AI use amplified exhaustion. Building AI readiness appears to be a personal resource that buffers against burnout.

**Try this today:** This week, notice when you find yourself comparing your output or pace to colleagues (especially around AI tool use), and redirect that energy toward a concrete skill-building action — such as completing one short tutorial on a work-relevant AI tool — to shift from comparison to competence-building.

## 6. When violence meets leadership: Leadership climate as a buffer in the link between violence, harmful alcohol use, and burnout among healthcare workers in Sweden.
*International journal of nursing studies advances, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42396545/)

**Summary:** A longitudinal study of nearly 2,500 Swedish healthcare workers found that a supportive leadership climate buffered the link between workplace violence and harmful alcohol use, and subsequently reduced the risk of burnout. The protective effect of good leadership was particularly evident under conditions of weaker support — suggesting leadership quality is a meaningful organizational lever even when workers face serious occupational hazards. Effect sizes were modest but theoretically consistent.

**Try this today:** If you are in a leadership role, schedule a brief one-on-one check-in this week with a team member who has faced a stressful or difficult situation at work — direct acknowledgment from a manager is one of the most accessible forms of the 'supportive leadership climate' shown to buffer burnout.

## 7. Vicarious trauma and burnout: the role of self-compassion.
*European journal of psychotraumatology, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42301724/)

**Summary:** A survey of 255 UK practitioner psychologists found that those with higher vicarious trauma scores also reported higher burnout across personal, work, and client-related dimensions, while higher self-compassion was associated with lower levels of both. Self-compassion did not statistically moderate the relationship between vicarious trauma and burnout overall, but self-kindness specifically showed a small buffering effect. The findings support compassion-focused approaches as potentially protective for workers exposed to others' distress.

**Try this today:** Practice a brief self-compassion micro-exercise today: after a difficult interaction or stressful task, spend 2 minutes acknowledging that the difficulty was real, that struggling is part of shared human experience, and offer yourself one kind, non-judgmental thought — this directly targets the self-kindness dimension linked to lower burnout.

## 8. A longitudinal network analysis of social connection and professional quality of life among healthcare workers during the COVID-19 pandemic.
*Journal of affective disorders, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42259489/)

**Summary:** A longitudinal network analysis of 230 US healthcare workers during the COVID-19 pandemic found that loneliness and positive mental health were the strongest drivers of downstream wellbeing outcomes, including burnout. Notably, giving support to others had a mutually reinforcing relationship with positive mental health — workers who gave support at one time point reported better mental health and work satisfaction later, which in turn predicted more giving. This suggests that being a source of support, not just a recipient, is itself a wellbeing resource.

**Try this today:** Proactively offer a small act of support to a colleague this week — a check-in message, sharing a useful resource, or helping with a task — framing it not as a cost to your energy but as an evidence-based investment in your own positive mental health and work satisfaction.
