## 1. Work-family conflict and mental health: A systematic review and meta-analysis.
*PLoS medicine, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42467702/)

**Summary:** This large meta-analysis of 52 longitudinal studies (112,714 participants) found that work-to-family conflict is independently associated with depressive symptoms, burnout, and general mental distress over time, even after controlling for baseline mental health. Family-to-work conflict similarly predicted depressive symptoms and burnout. These effects were consistent regardless of gender, geography, or follow-up length.

**Try this today:** This week, audit one concrete boundary between work and personal time—such as setting a hard stop on checking work email after a specific hour—and communicate it explicitly to your team or manager to reduce work-to-family spillover.

## 2. Artificial Intelligence and Clinician Burnout in the United States: A Narrative Review.
*Cureus, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42465700/)

**Summary:** This narrative review of US evidence found that AI tools (especially scribes) show early promise in reducing documentation time and may modestly improve clinician well-being, but often 'displace' rather than eliminate work—shifting tasks from physicians to nurses or from production to oversight. The authors propose a 'displacement of burden' framework to evaluate AI implementation effects on burnout.

**Try this today:** Before adopting any new AI productivity tool at work, spend 15 minutes mapping out which tasks it will actually eliminate versus which it will merely move to a different role or step—use this to have a more informed conversation with your team about whether the tool genuinely reduces workload.

## 3. The relationship between job burnout and emotional intelligence among employees of teaching hospitals affiliated with Abadan University of Medical Sciences, Iran (2024).
*BMC health services research, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42464315/)

**Summary:** In a cross-sectional study of 325 hospital employees in Iran, 30% showed high burnout levels and 70% moderate burnout. Higher emotional intelligence was significantly associated with lower job burnout, with each unit increase in emotional intelligence linked to a measurable decrease in burnout scores.

**Try this today:** Practice one emotional intelligence micro-skill today: before responding to a frustrating work message or situation, take 60 seconds to label the specific emotion you are feeling (e.g., 'I feel dismissed' rather than 'I feel bad'), which research links to better emotional regulation and reduced stress reactivity.

## 4. From Turnover to Transition: Lived Experiences of Former Pharmacy Faculty Who Left Academia.
*American journal of pharmaceutical education, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42463020/)

**Summary:** Semi-structured interviews with 16 former pharmacy faculty who left academia revealed that departure was driven by cumulative, interconnected stressors including workload strain, loss of autonomy, administrative instability, and mentorship gaps—not single isolated incidents. For some, leaving was a forced disengagement; for others, an intentional, self-directed career transition.

**Try this today:** Reflect on whether your current workplace dissatisfaction feels like cumulative drift or a values-driven signal—write down three specific organizational conditions (not personal failings) that are contributing to your stress, which can help clarify whether to advocate for changes internally or begin planning a proactive transition.

## 5. Biological Markers of Burnout Severity: A Systematic Review.
*Stress and health : journal of the International Society for the Investigation of Stress, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42452902/)

**Summary:** This systematic review of 111 studies and 36 biomarkers found no consistent physiological signature for burnout syndrome. While HbA1c and blood glucose showed relatively more consistent associations, overall biological evidence is insufficient to support routine clinical diagnosis of burnout via biomarkers.

**Try this today:** Rather than waiting for objective biological 'proof' of burnout to take action, use a validated self-report tool this week (such as the Maslach Burnout Inventory or the Oldenburg Burnout Inventory, both freely available for personal use) to get a structured snapshot of your current exhaustion and disengagement levels.

## 6. Drivers of Professional Satisfaction in Emergency Medicine Residency Training in the US.
*AEM education and training, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42428348/)

**Summary:** A national survey of nearly 4,000 US emergency medicine residents found that the top drivers of professional satisfaction were team culture and community, making a positive patient impact, and work-life balance—not pay or prestige. These findings were consistent across gender, race, and training year.

**Try this today:** Identify one specific aspect of your work culture or team dynamic that gives you energy (e.g., a particular collaboration, a type of case, a colleague's working style) and deliberately schedule or protect time for more of that activity this week.

## 7. Evolving Well-Being and Personal Resource Profiles Following a Smartphone App Intervention for Stress Management.
*Stress and health : journal of the International Society for the Investigation of Stress, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42411755/)

**Summary:** A three-week study using a stress-management smartphone app found that workers with the highest stress and lowest coping resources were most likely to shift into better-functioning profiles when using the app daily, while the control group's profiles remained stable. Changes happened gradually, moving between adjacent profiles rather than in dramatic leaps.

**Try this today:** Download a structured stress-management app (such as Headspace, Woebot, or Calm) and commit to using it for just 10 minutes daily for one week—the evidence suggests that those who are most stressed stand to gain the most from consistent use.

## 8. When violence meets leadership: Leadership climate as a buffer in the link between violence, harmful alcohol use, and burnout among healthcare workers in Sweden.
*International journal of nursing studies advances, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42396545/)

**Summary:** A two-wave longitudinal study of 2,446 Swedish healthcare workers found that exposure to workplace violence increased harmful alcohol use and subsequent burnout, but a strong leadership climate significantly weakened this pathway. Under strong leadership, the indirect link from violence to burnout via alcohol use became non-significant.

**Try this today:** If you have experienced any workplace hostility or aggression recently, proactively schedule a brief check-in with your manager this week—not necessarily to report the incident formally, but to maintain a connection with supportive leadership, which the data suggests buffers stress-driven coping behaviors.

## 9. Occupational burnout and its association with physical activity and cardiorespiratory fitness among nurses: a narrative review.
*Medycyna pracy, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42367094/)

**Summary:** This narrative review of studies on nurses found that physical activity is consistently inversely associated with burnout—particularly emotional exhaustion—with some evidence of a dose-response relationship. However, most evidence relies on self-reported activity, and the role of objectively measured cardiorespiratory fitness remains underexplored.

**Try this today:** Add a minimum 20-minute moderate-intensity physical activity (brisk walk, cycling, or similar) to at least three days this week and treat it as a non-negotiable work-recovery appointment in your calendar rather than an optional extra.

## 10. Pathways linking emergency department work environment to nurse's burnout: The mediating effects of teamwork and workload.
*International emergency nursing, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42364448/)

**Summary:** A cross-sectional survey of 156 emergency nurses in South Korea found that a better nursing work environment reduced burnout both directly and indirectly by improving teamwork and reducing perceived workload. Teamwork and workload each independently mediated the environment-burnout relationship.

**Try this today:** Identify one specific workload bottleneck on your team this week—a recurring task that feels disproportionately draining—and raise it in your next team meeting with a concrete suggestion for redistribution or process improvement, treating workload management as a shared organizational responsibility.

## 11. Paid Time Off Pressure in Emergency Medicine: Vacation Conflict Risk in the Context of Group Size, and Shift Coverage.
*The Journal of emergency medicine, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42361787/)

**Summary:** Using probabilistic modeling, this study quantifies 'PTO pressure'—the likelihood that a physician's vacation request will conflict with scheduling limits—showing it rises sharply in smaller teams with generous leave entitlements. Unresolved scheduling conflicts are identified as a meaningful contributor to physician dissatisfaction and burnout.

**Try this today:** If you manage a team, review your current time-off policy this week to check whether your group size and coverage rules create structural conflict risk—even one additional person approved for concurrent leave at a time can meaningfully reduce pressure for smaller teams.
