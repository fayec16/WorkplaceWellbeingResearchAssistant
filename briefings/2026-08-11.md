## 1. Factors associated with burnout among public hospital nurses in South Korea: A Job Demands-Resources perspective.
*PloS one, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42574443/)

**Summary:** A study of 206 nurses in South Korean public hospitals found that higher workload was the strongest predictor of burnout, while mindfulness practice and a relationship-oriented organizational culture were independently associated with lower burnout. The model explained 58% of variance in burnout scores, suggesting both job demands and personal resources matter. Notably, coworker support alone did not independently reduce burnout after controlling for other factors.

**Try this today:** This week, try a brief mindfulness practice (even 5-10 minutes of focused breathing before or after your highest-demand work period) and notice whether it changes how depleted you feel at day's end — consistent with the finding that mindfulness independently buffers burnout even under heavy workloads.

## 2. Cumulative Lifetime Trauma Exposure Among Employed Mental Health Care Professionals and Associations With Professional Quality of Life: A Cross-Sectional Survey.
*Journal of psychiatric and mental health nursing, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42573203/)

**Summary:** A survey of 309 mental health care professionals in Denmark found that cumulative lifetime trauma exposure — counting distinct types of traumatic events experienced — was independently associated with higher burnout and secondary traumatic stress at work, beyond workplace exposures alone. This 'invisible' personal history affected 34% of staff who reported two or more trauma categories. The findings support universal trauma-informed organizational approaches that don't require staff to disclose personal trauma.

**Try this today:** If you manage or influence HR policy, propose replacing targeted mental health screening (which requires disclosure) with universal, low-barrier wellbeing supports like structured debrief sessions or opt-out peer support programs that benefit all employees regardless of personal history.

## 3. We Give a Shit: A Commentary on the Psychometric Properties of the Organizational Bullshit Perception Scale.
*Psychological reports, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42571991/)

**Summary:** Two studies with over 2,300 employees in Germany examined perceived 'organizational bullshit' — workplace communication with no regard for truth — and found associations with employee wellbeing. The studies also surfaced measurement challenges, suggesting the construct is real and workplace-relevant but not yet cleanly operationalized. Exposure to meaningless or dishonest organizational communication appears to be a genuine stressor worth taking seriously.

**Try this today:** Audit one recurring meeting or communication channel this week: identify whether any regular messages or updates lack clear purpose or factual grounding, and consider either eliminating them or replacing them with transparent, concrete updates — reducing your team's exposure to organizational noise.

## 4. Occupational Stress in the Neuroticism-Burnout Link: An Exploratory Examination of Physiological Contributions.
*Clinical psychopharmacology and neuroscience : the official scientific journal of the Korean College of Neuropsychopharmacology, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42528350/)

**Summary:** A study of 150 Korean workers in high-stress occupations found that neuroticism predicts burnout primarily through perceived occupational stress — meaning stress perception is the key mediating mechanism, not personality alone. Wearable physiological measures (EEG, heart rate variability) directionally supported a link between reduced autonomic flexibility and burnout but did not reach statistical significance. Age was inversely related to burnout, suggesting experience may be protective.

**Try this today:** Because perceived stress — not just actual workload — drives burnout, try reappraising one high-pressure task this week by writing down specifically what is and isn't within your control about it, a simple cognitive technique shown to reduce stress perception.

## 5. Mental health first aid in the workplace: a reflexive thematic analysis of UK workers' experiences.
*International journal of qualitative studies on health and well-being, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42482327/)

**Summary:** A qualitative study embedded in a UK randomized trial found that Mental Health First Aid (MHFA) programs in workplaces suffer from ambiguity about their purpose, employee scepticism about effectiveness, and reluctance to disclose mental health concerns due to stigma and fears about career impact. Participants noted MHFA worked best when embedded in a broader organizational commitment to wellbeing rather than as a standalone initiative. Addressing root organizational causes of distress was seen as more important than peer support alone.

**Try this today:** If your organization has a Mental Health First Aider program, check this week whether there is clear, accessible communication about what MHFA is and is not (including confidentiality boundaries) — if not, draft a one-page explainer to reduce ambiguity and encourage uptake.

## 6. Social comparison contributes to work exhaustion in the context of workplace AI use: A three-wave follow-up study of Finnish workers.
*SSM - population health, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42471855/)

**Summary:** A three-wave longitudinal study of over 2,100 Finnish workers found that frequent AI use at work itself was not associated with work exhaustion, but strong tendencies to compare oneself socially with colleagues were robustly linked to higher exhaustion at both individual and population levels. Importantly, among employees who frequently compare themselves to others, heavy AI use at work was associated with elevated exhaustion — suggesting AI may amplify social comparison pressures. Workers who felt more ready for AI reported lower exhaustion.

**Try this today:** This week, notice moments when you compare your AI-assisted productivity or outputs to colleagues' and consciously redirect that energy toward your own progress benchmarks — given that social comparison tendency, not AI use per se, is the key driver of exhaustion in AI-augmented workplaces.

## 7. When violence meets leadership: Leadership climate as a buffer in the link between violence, harmful alcohol use, and burnout among healthcare workers in Sweden.
*International journal of nursing studies advances, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42396545/)

**Summary:** A two-wave longitudinal study of 2,446 Swedish healthcare workers found that workplace violence predicted harmful alcohol use, which in turn predicted later burnout. Crucially, a strong leadership climate significantly buffered the link between violence and alcohol use, and the indirect path from violence to burnout through alcohol use was only significant under weak leadership conditions. The findings suggest supportive leadership can interrupt a maladaptive stress-coping chain.

**Try this today:** If you are in a leadership role, schedule at least one brief, informal individual check-in with a team member this week specifically focused on how they are coping with workplace stressors — not just task progress — since consistent supportive supervisor contact appears to buffer harmful coping responses.

## 8. Vicarious trauma and burnout: the role of self-compassion.
*European journal of psychotraumatology, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42301724/)

**Summary:** A survey of 255 UK practitioner psychologists found that vicarious trauma from client work was associated with higher burnout and lower self-compassion, with higher self-compassion linked to lower levels of both vicarious trauma and burnout. While self-compassion did not statistically moderate the trauma-burnout relationship, self-kindness showed a small buffering effect. The findings suggest compassion-focused approaches may help professionals who regularly absorb others' distress.

**Try this today:** At the end of a difficult workday involving emotionally demanding interactions, try a brief self-compassion pause: place a hand on your chest, acknowledge that what you experienced was hard, and remind yourself that struggling in response to others' pain is a shared human experience — a simple evidence-linked practice to reduce burnout risk.
