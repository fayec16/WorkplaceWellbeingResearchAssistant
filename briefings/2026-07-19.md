## 1. Feasibility and acceptability of a joint employee-manager workplace intervention to support mental health and job retention (MENTOR 2.0).
*BMC public health, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42471615/)

**Summary:** The MENTOR 2.0 intervention tested a joint employee-manager approach to workplace mental health, involving four individual sessions and one joint session facilitated by a trained liaison worker. Results from 23 employees and 19 managers showed high feasibility (94% session completion, 87% retention) and preliminary improvements in psychological distress, productivity, work engagement, and psychological flexibility. The study highlights that mental health support works better when it addresses the relationship between employee and manager rather than treating employees in isolation.

**Try this today:** Schedule a brief, structured one-on-one with your direct manager this week—frame it explicitly as a check-in on workload, role clarity, and what support you need—mirroring the relational dialogue this intervention showed to be effective.

## 2. Factors associated with quiet quitting among public health specialists in Trkiye: a cross-sectional study.
*BMC public health, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42471649/)

**Summary:** A cross-sectional study of 241 public health specialists in Turkey found that quiet quitting—passive disengagement and minimal work effort—was strongly associated with having no administrative role, a hopeless outlook on career prospects, and a mismatch between specialist training and actual job duties. About 28% of respondents were considering resigning, and those with role-education mismatches were far more likely to disengage. The findings suggest that when workers feel their skills are wasted or their career has no future, disengagement follows even without formal resignation.

**Try this today:** Audit how well your current tasks use your core skills—list three things you were trained or hired to do that you rarely get to practice, and bring one specific reallocation suggestion to your manager in your next meeting.

## 3. Work-family conflict and mental health: A systematic review and meta-analysis.
*PLoS medicine, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42467702/)

**Summary:** A large systematic review and meta-analysis of 52 longitudinal studies (112,714 participants) found that work-to-family conflict independently predicts depressive symptoms, burnout, and general mental distress over time, even after controlling for baseline mental health. Family-to-work conflict similarly predicted depression and burnout. Crucially, these effects held regardless of gender or geographic region, suggesting the work-family interface is a universal burnout risk factor.

**Try this today:** Identify one concrete boundary between work and family time that has eroded recently—such as checking work messages during dinner—and implement a specific, scheduled cutoff time for work communications starting tonight.

## 4. Biological Markers of Burnout Severity: A Systematic Review.
*Stress and health : journal of the International Society for the Investigation of Stress, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42452902/)

**Summary:** A systematic review of 111 studies examining 36 biomarkers found no consistent physiological signature for burnout syndrome; most markers including cortisol showed inconclusive associations. Blood glucose and HbA1c showed slightly more consistent links but evidence remains insufficient for clinical diagnosis. The findings underscore that burnout cannot yet be detected through routine biological testing and must be identified through behavioral and psychological assessment.

**Try this today:** Use a validated self-report burnout screener (e.g., the free Oldenburg Burnout Inventory available online) this week to get a concrete baseline score, since biological markers are not yet reliable indicators—track it monthly to spot trends before they worsen.

## 5. Evolving Well-Being and Personal Resource Profiles Following a Smartphone App Intervention for Stress Management.
*Stress and health : journal of the International Society for the Investigation of Stress, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42411755/)

**Summary:** A three-week study using the WellBe! smartphone app for stress management found that users who were struggling the most benefited most from the digital intervention, showing gradual transitions to better coping profiles. Changes were incremental rather than dramatic, with shifts between adjacent well-being states. The control group showed stable profiles, suggesting even a brief, consistent digital intervention can move the needle for those under the most strain.

**Try this today:** Download one evidence-based stress management app (e.g., Calm, Headspace, or a CBT-based tool) and commit to using it daily for three weeks—setting a specific, recurring 10-minute time slot to build the habit.

## 6. When violence meets leadership: Leadership climate as a buffer in the link between violence, harmful alcohol use, and burnout among healthcare workers in Sweden.
*International journal of nursing studies advances, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42396545/)

**Summary:** A longitudinal panel study of 2,446 healthcare workers in Sweden found that a strong leadership climate buffered the link between workplace violence and harmful alcohol use, and consequently reduced downstream burnout risk. Under weak leadership conditions, violence led to alcohol misuse which then predicted later burnout; this pathway was not significant under strong leadership. The results suggest that supportive supervisory behavior is a meaningful organizational lever even in high-stress, high-risk work environments.

**Try this today:** If you manage others, identify one concrete behavior this week that signals psychological safety—such as explicitly thanking a team member for raising a concern or blocking time to check in on workload—since supervisory support demonstrably breaks harmful stress-coping chains.

## 7. Mental Workload and Burnout among Social Work Professionals in Spain: The Protective Role of Psychological Capital and Perceived Social Support.
*Health & social work, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42276977/)

**Summary:** A study of 270 Spanish social workers found that high mental workload directly predicts burnout, and that both psychological capital (optimism, resilience, self-efficacy) and perceived social support only partially buffer this relationship—meaning workload itself still needs to be addressed. Both internal resources and external support networks act as independent protective factors against burnout. The findings argue for dual-track interventions targeting both personal coping resources and the social environment.

**Try this today:** This week, proactively reach out to one colleague or professional peer for a genuine work-related conversation—not task-focused, but focused on shared challenges—since perceived social support is an independently protective factor against burnout regardless of workload level.

## 8. Burnout and Work-Life Balance: A Longitudinal Study Into the Transition From Medical School to Postgraduate Training.
*Deutsches Arzteblatt international, 2026* — via PubMed — [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42273790/)

**Summary:** A longitudinal study tracking German medical students through their practical year and into postgraduate training found that emotional exhaustion jumped from 10% to 47% of participants over that transition period, while work-life balance steadily declined at every stage. Depersonalization was already alarmingly high (44%) even in early medical school. The study demonstrates that burnout is not a sudden event but an accumulating trajectory, making early intervention critical.

**Try this today:** Treat your current work-life balance as a leading indicator rather than a lagging one—schedule a 15-minute personal review at the end of this week to honestly assess whether your energy, recovery time, and boundaries have deteriorated compared to three months ago, and identify one specific change to make next week.
